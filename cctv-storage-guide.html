How to Calculate CCTV Storage Requirements: Formulas, Worked Examples & HDD Sizing

Plan IP camera and NVR recording capacity using actual bitrates, retention days and recording schedules—not resolution alone. This guide explains the calculations, their assumptions, and the checks required before selecting surveillance hard drives.

Published 20 November 2026 · Technical reference · All capacity figures use decimal GB/TB unless stated otherwise

Quick answer: For 16 cameras averaging 4 Mbps each, recording continuously for 30 days, base footage is 20.74 TB . Adding a 10% planning allowance gives 22.81 TB of required nominal capacity before RAID, filesystem and reserved-space deductions. Check with the free calculator .

1. What determines CCTV storage?

Storage is primarily a function of the number of streams , each stream's average recorded bitrate , and actual recording time . Resolution, frames per second (FPS), compression codec, scene motion, image noise, lighting and camera settings influence bitrate, but do not uniquely determine it.

A 4MP camera might generate very different file sizes when viewing a quiet corridor versus a busy road at night. For reliable planning, obtain representative average bitrate measurements from the camera or NVR, including busy and nighttime periods. A configured maximum bitrate is a conservative planning input, not necessarily the measured average.

2. CCTV storage calculation formula

Network camera bitrate is commonly specified in megabits per second (Mbps) , while disk capacity is specified in gigabytes or terabytes . Divide by eight to convert bits to bytes. The equations below assume a constant representative average bitrate during recorded time.

Daily storage (GB) = N × B × 3,600 × H × F ÷ 8,000 N = camera count; B = average recorded Mbps per camera; H = scheduled recording hours/day; F = recorded-time fraction (1 for continuous, e.g. 0.5 for 50% event duty cycle)

For the total recording period:

Required capacity (TB) = N × B × 3,600 × H × F × D ÷ 8,000,000 × (1 + A/100) D = retention days; A = extra planning allowance percentage. This is decimal TB and does not account for RAID usable-capacity losses.

Why 8,000,000? There are 8 bits per byte and 1,000,000,000,000 bytes per decimal TB. With bitrate in megabits per second (10⁶ bits/s), the combined conversion factor is 8,000,000.

Step-by-step: 16 cameras for 30 days

  1. Count: 16 IP cameras.
  2. Bitrate: 4 Mbps representative average recorded bitrate per camera.
  3. Schedule: 24 hours/day, continuous (F = 1).
  4. Retention: 30 days.
  5. Allowance: 10%.

Daily recording = 16 × 4 × 3,600 × 24 ÷ 8,000 = 691.20 GB/day .

30-day base storage = 691.20 × 30 ÷ 1,000 = 20.736 TB .

With 10% allowance = 20.736 × 1.10 = 22.8096 TB ≈ 22.81 TB .

Engineering caution: 22.81 TB is a planning requirement, not a guarantee that a particular combination of hard drives will provide 30 days. Verify NVR drive limits, actual usable volume, reserved space, stream selection, RAID and operating conditions.

4. Resolution, FPS and bitrate: what changes?

Resolution is the number of pixels per frame; FPS is the number of frames recorded each second; bitrate is the amount of encoded video data transmitted per second. A higher resolution or FPS often requires more bitrate at similar quality, but modern encoders adjust compression according to content and settings.

5. H.264 vs H.265 vs smart codecs

H.265/HEVC can deliver similar perceived video quality at lower bitrate than H.264/AVC under suitable conditions, but the savings depend on encoder implementation, content, quality targets and settings. Manufacturer-specific H.264+ and H.265+ smart codecs can use scene-adaptive methods; there is no universal, defensible fixed savings percentage across all camera models and scenes.

For the most accurate result, configure each camera with the intended codec, resolution, FPS and quality settings, then measure the bitrate over a representative interval. When only a heuristic is available, label it clearly as an estimate and include a margin.

6. Continuous vs motion/event recording

Continuous recording stores video throughout the scheduled window. Event recording stores video when a trigger is active, often with pre-event and post-event clips. Storage can be modeled using a recorded-time fraction , but this fraction must be estimated from real operating conditions.

For example, a system that actually records 12 hours of video per camera per 24-hour day has F = 0.5. Under otherwise identical assumptions, its footage capacity is roughly half of continuous recording. However, alarm frequency, clip overlap, pre/post recording and different bitrates during activity can make real-world results depart from this simplification.

Do not assume: Selecting motion recording automatically saves 50% or 80% of space. Validate event duty cycle using NVR recording logs and a representative sample of days.

7. Selecting surveillance HDD capacity and RAID

First calculate footage requirements; then translate that requirement into usable recording capacity . A marketed 8 TB drive has 8 trillion bytes of nominal capacity, while operating systems may report capacity in tebibytes (TiB), giving a smaller numeric display. Filesystem, metadata, reserved space, bad-sector handling and recorder configuration can further affect usable capacity.

For RAID, raw disk capacity and usable capacity are not equal. As a simplified illustration for equal-size drives: RAID 1 mirrors data (about one drive's capacity in a two-drive mirror); RAID 5 generally uses the equivalent of one drive for parity; RAID 6 generally uses two drives' worth for parity. Exact availability depends on implementation and constraints. RAID is not a backup.

Practical HDD selection checklist

  1. Verify the NVR's supported number of HDD bays and maximum capacity per drive.
  2. Confirm whether RAID is available, enabled and supported for the chosen drives.
  3. Check surveillance workload rating, recording duty cycle and drive compatibility.
  4. Confirm available space after formatting, reserved capacity and recorder overhead.
  5. Test actual recording duration and overwrite behavior after installation.

8. How many days will a 1 TB, 2 TB or 4 TB HDD record?

Rearrange the formula to estimate retention days:

Days ≈ Usable capacity (TB) × 8,000,000 ÷ (N × B × 3,600 × H × F) Capacity is decimal TB available for video. No additional allowance is applied here; account for overhead before using this equation.

Example: one camera at 4 Mbps recording 24/7 uses 43.2 GB/day . Ignoring overhead, 1 TB holds about 23.1 days ; 2 TB about 46.3 days ; 4 TB about 92.6 days . With four cameras at the same bitrate, those durations are about one-quarter as long.

9. Common sizing mistakes

  • Assuming megapixels equal a fixed bitrate: They do not.
  • Mixing Mbps and MB/s: One byte contains eight bits.
  • Confusing TB and TiB: Decimal drive labels and binary OS reporting differ.
  • Ignoring night conditions: Noise and activity can increase bitrate.
  • Assuming smart-codec savings: Validate model- and scene-specific performance.
  • Using raw RAID capacity: Calculate usable volume instead.
  • Forgetting additional streams or audio: Include every recorded data source.
  • Overlooking retention policy: Some sites have regulatory or contractual minimums.

10. Frequently asked questions

How much storage does a 4MP CCTV camera need per day?

There is no single fixed number. At an illustrative average of 4 Mbps and 24/7 recording, one camera uses 43.2 GB/day (decimal). Use the camera's actual recorded bitrate for better accuracy.

Does H.265 always use half the storage of H.264?

No. Savings vary by encoder, scene, quality target and camera settings. A 50% reduction is not a universal guarantee.

How much storage do 16 cameras need for 30 days?

At 4 Mbps each, 24/7, the base estimate is 20.736 TB. With 10% planning allowance, approximately 22.81 TB before RAID and filesystem deductions.

Can I use this formula for an NVR with motion recording?

Yes, as a planning model, by multiplying recorded hours by an estimated event duty fraction. Actual clip patterns and bitrates should be verified.

Why is my 8 TB hard drive shown as less than 8 TB?

Drive makers label capacity in decimal TB, while many operating systems display binary units (TiB) and may reserve space for filesystem structures.

Conclusion

Reliable CCTV storage planning starts with recorded average bitrate and retention requirements . Calculate the base video volume, add a documented allowance, and verify the usable HDD capacity after RAID and recorder overhead. Treat resolution-based bitrate values as preliminary estimates until measured or confirmed by the manufacturer.
